# **The Game** 
Soul Sworn is a card game that was meant to be a printed, physical card game. The webapp is (in general) designed to allow you the same flexibility as having a real deck of cards (with exceptions to short-cut repetative tasks like dealing, or to allow some actions in a window that would normally only be possible on a table). As such, there are many ways to play, but the primary rule-set going with the Pass the Storyteller method.

- Player 2 Character Card Slot - for the Player 1 character card

- Player 2 Hand - 5 card slots
- Game Rules Tray - a container that slides out from the left of the game board; the jpg on the background of that container is an infographic of the rules
- Notes Tray - a container that slides out from the right of the game board; this functions as a basic text editor where the player can keep notes; these notes persist
- Card Inspector - where large-scale cards appear when clicked (so players can read them better)
- Story Grid - 10x4 grid of card slots where players will play most of their cards
- Mutable Column - 1x4 column of card slots that could be used for a variety of things
- Main Deck Draw Pile - where any cards go that were unassigned to player hands, or the alt deck; main deck never contains character cards or objective cards

## PASS THE STORYTELLER

**The Storyteller**
In this version of play, the Storyteller, the player that manages the scene, changes depending on the ideas offered and how the players vote. The Storyteller runs any NPCs, Monsters, magic, and global events, decides if any role-play is plausible, attempts to maintain the agreed-upon-path (see Play Sequence), and may demand Skilled Action tests. 

As an alternative, the group may decide to allow NPCs to be role-played by the player that introduced the NPC, but success or failure of any NPC actions is up to the Storyteller. Because NPCs do not have stats in Soul Sworn, skilled actions are not tested.

While the rules exist to structure the story, always PRIORITIZE THE STORY. If there are rules that do not work for you, change them.

**Skilled Actions**
The core randomizing element of Soul Sworn is the Skilled Action.
A skilled action is any action taken by a character for which there is a significant risk of failure. Skilled actions may include (but are not limited to):

    - PHYSICAL feats of strength or dexterity such as climbing something, kicking down a door, wrestling, knife fighting, stealing a wallet, running to catch an opponent, etc.
    - EMOTIONAL feats of charisma and intuition such as persuasion, soothing an anxious person, connecting with the spirit world, overcoming vices and impulses, assessing a threat, etc.
    - RATIONAL feats of cleverness or problem-solving such as casting a spell, code breaking, doing math, using tech, drawing on trivial knowledge, etc.

Some skilled actions may be combinations, requiring success in two attributes. Picking a lock might require Rational and Physical skill. Surviving a psychic blast might require Physical and Emotional skill. Martial arts might require success in all three areas.

If there is any doubt as to which attributes are tested in a skilled action, the group may vote on it, but the active Storyteller makes the final call.

## The Setup

- Player One, the player hosting/operating the game board, is The Dealer. The Dealer sets up the round, number of players, determines the Objective Card (or setting a custom objective), the game length, etc.

- The Dealer sets the Advantage Tokens on the character cards, typically following the printed prerequisites on the cards. After the prerequisites tokens are placed, the players use the remaining tokens to determine how to balance their characters between Rational, Emotional, and Physical advantages.

- The Dealer selects a card to play as the Origin Card, placing it into the first card slot on the story grid. (This can be from their hand, the top of the deck, or selected from the card browser).

- The Dealer is the first Storyteller, establishing the beginning of the story with an opening narrative related to the first card. This will motivate the characters to immediate action, moving them toward the objects. Often this narrative will communicate the stakes, what's on the line if the characters don't meet the objective. Unless you really like a challenge, it is recommended the dealer pre-select the Origin and Objective.

- Play continues to Player Two to lay a card for the next scene, and so-on round-robin.

**Default Party Premise**
Soul Sworn is not structured for in-game play of characters meeting for the first time.  The party is formed at the outset.
While the dealer can still define how the characters met in the opening narration, you can default to the concept of Found Family and the relationship complexity that comes with that, or Contracted Party which is the more standard opening moves for adventure TTRPGs.

## Play Sequence

**PLAY A CARD:**
- The active player selects a card from their hand and places it in the next card slot on the story grid.

- ALL PLAYERS write down their idea for what happens in the scene, the scene proposal, driving the characters toward the final objective. Ideas must incorporate the card details (literally or in spirit), and the idea must incorporate at least one skilled action for their character.

**ACT ONE:**
- Players vote on which scene proposal seems the best, and the winner becomes the Storyteller, taking over ownership of the scene up to the moment their character attempts a skilled action. Other players may jump in, playing their own characters, but the Storyteller is the architect and arbiter of Act One.

- Failing a skilled action test in Act One results in the Storyteller describing the failure outcomes and could potentially derail the intended path of the scene. This is entirely at the Storyteller’s discretion.

- Act One ends when the Storyteller’s character attempts a skilled action, any skilled action, whether it was the action proposed in the initial path or not.

**INTERVAL - THE SKILLED ACTION TEST:**
- This is the test to determine if the scene moves forward (roughly) according to plan, or if the scene goes… sideways.
Determine which attribute(s) the skilled action is testing. Each attribute is tested individually. All tests must succeed to count as a SUCCESS OUTCOME.

- Click the d20 to test skilled action success. Add any advantage points from the associated attribute (the number of advantage points is determine by the token count set at the beginning of the game).

- If a d20 roll plus advantage points is 12 or greater, the skilled action is a success. If a d20 roll plus advantage points is 11 or less, the skilled action fails. Regardless of advantage points, a natural 20 roll is an extreme success, and natural 1 roll is an extreme failure.

**INTERVAL RESOLUTION:**
- For a success, the active Storyteller describes the success and carries the story out through Act Two, arbitrating the other characters’s actions, etc.

- For an extreme success, the action was executed with such mastery that dramatic, cinematic outcomes can be described, even pushing the bounds of probability.

- For a failure, THE NEXT PLAYER IN PLAY ORDER becomes the Storyteller, describes the failure scenario, and takes over the scene for Act Two.

- For an extreme failure, the action was bumbled so badly that dramatic, harmful, maybe hilarious, maybe deadly, outcomes can be described.

**ACT TWO:** 
- This is the resolution of the scene, ideally continuing to role-play the scenario until either the scene reaches a natural ending, or the scene gets stale and a new card needs to be played. The players will generally agree when a scene is over, but the Storyteller makes the final call.

- If the characters change location, this ALMOST ALWAYS counts as the end of a scene. Be prepared to defend an in-scene location change to the rest of the group.

-After the scene ends, THE NEXT PLAYER IN PLAY ORDER drops a new card from their hand into the story grid, and the next scene begins.

*NOTE ON SPELL CARDS:*
Spells are a slightly different animal than the other cards. While they could be used to prompt a new scene, they were conceived to be played at any time during a scene. They have an effect and a duration. If your dealer includes spell cards in the main deck, consider playing the spell cards in the mutable column (described in Flexible Game Board) until they expire. If you are not running a 40-scene game (if you are, you’re an animal), then you could also keep these in the bottom row of the story grid for their duration.

*A NOTE ON CHARACTER DEATH:*
The Obscura is a gruesome place. Things don’t end well, generally, for anyone in particular. Characters might die.

It is up to the PLAYER to decide if their character dies in a scenario, and not the Storyteller. If a player wants to hang on, or even take on ghostly aspects, this should be respected and worked into the story.

If a player agrees their character has died, the player continues to drop cards, make path suggestions, and act as Storyteller. They are no longer required to include a skilled action in their scene proposals. And for any scenes for which they have won ownership, there is no demarcation between Acts One and Two, and they will maintain ownership until the end of the scene.

**End Game**
The game ends when the Objective card has been reached; the Objective card represents the climax of the story.

- If the players managed to narrate a causal, plausible story arc from the Origin to the Objective, reaching the highest moment of tension and action for the Objective scene, the game has been “won”. The Objective NEED NOT BE MET for a game win. Tragedies are a thing.

- If the players could not reach the final climactic scene, the game has been “lost”. You are defeated and you’ve brought shame upon yourself and your house.

- Regardless of winning or losing, completing the game opens the superlatives panel. Players will accrue various awards. The player with the most awards is named The Eidolon and has the honor of denouement.

- The denouement (aka conclusion, aka resolution,) is narrated by The Eidolon.

**Flexible Game Board**
- The game board was designed to allow you to handle the cards like a real deck, as much as possible. There are some assumption made and shortcuts built-in to keep things manageable, but the intro and outro sequences are minimal, maximizing your ability to modify play.

- With the exception of the deck/discard areas, you can click-drag cards wherever you like.

- The mutable column is comprised of the four card slots not attached to the story grid. You can use these for… kind of whatever.

- The alternative deck can be configured with any card types in the game, except for character cards (those are still available through the browser). In a version where the group decides on a more challenging play sequence, the alt deck could be loaded with location cards, forcing a top card draw to change the scene location. Players would then lay their hand as part of selected scene proposal. This could also be loaded with Spells, for a story where magic has gone wild. One of the most challenging modes of play would be the use of Sub-Objectives, in which you’d load the alt deck with objective cards and have the players select a sub-objective they are trying to meet.
